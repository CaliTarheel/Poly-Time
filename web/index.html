Rift inheritance +

Ordinary continental crust (not cratons, not ocean) starts with a smooth seeded pattern of inherited weakness. At the default 0.25 it is up to 25% weaker, about 12.5% on average. Rift damage leaves a durable scar of up to 50% weaker that outlasts the damage itself, so crust that has broken breaks again. The length scale sets the pattern size: at 350 km, points 350 km apart are still similar, the pattern is unrelated by about 1,100 km (about 550 pixels on a 2 km/pixel map), and weak and strong regions alternate over roughly 1,000–1,500 km. Every world with the same weakness seed gets the same pattern; change the seed for a different inheritance. Craton interiors stay rigid behind a deformable rim (150 km, about 75 pixels). Rupture is judged on realized stretching of material links against a numerical breakup gate (hand-set, under review) rather than the coarse loading proxy. Enhanced rifting alone does not cure slow continental breakup.

How island arcs form

Where an oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate, released water helps mantle rock melt. Magma rises through the overriding plate: oceanic crust can develop an island arc; continental crust can develop a continental volcanic arc. USGS illustration ↗

Here, new arc material accumulates on oceanic crust of the overriding plate, roughly 180 km behind a convergent boundary. It can be tracked before enough material builds up to appear on the crust map.

Crust behind the volcanic arc can also stretch. If the arc pulls away from the plate's interior, this extension can develop into a back-arc basin and eventually new seafloor. It does not happen at every trench. Evidence from the Aleutian Basin ↗ Recorded basin phases and opening histories appear below the regional inspector.

What the model records

The revised physics setup treats normal convergence independently of sideways sliding. Incoming ocean and recorded polarity determine whether subduction and arc growth occur. Lite treats declared starting trenches as established; detailed slab experiments build maturity with sustained convergence over at least 10 Myr and 100 km of shortening. Legacy experiments retain their saved selection rules. Added buoyant reference area is (convergence + 8) × trench length × time step × 0.015 × trench maturity, in km² with speed in km/Myr and time in Myr. Detailed fixed-trench experiments can include a bounded retreat contribution; Lite uses actual convergence without that transfer. Arc crust occupies its actual moving footprint, with connected volcanic cores, sloping flanks, and submerged aprons. An island emerges where that surface rises above sea level; small islands can be missed by a coarse review map. Overlapping arc surfaces expose the highest surface present. Existing arcs can grow, gain magma, and deform where mechanically connected or in collision.

An arc event records the first addition for that parent plate within each 100 Myr interval. Its area is the addition in that one integration step, not an interval total or a volcano count. Back-arc records distinguish loading, rifting, spreading, quiet intervals and closure. Loading alone is not measured opening; the saved extension and phase changes show what occurred. Lite loads a back-arc sector when the overriding plate's solved motion carries it away from an active declared trench during oceanic convergence, measured in the stationary mantle frame. This needs no slab depth or mass threshold and adds no independent rollback speed or driving force on the overriding plate. Existing arc, sector, rupture and basin geometry retain their detail. Detailed experiments retain their recorded retreat or anchored-slab loading laws. These rules do not explicitly solve water or temperature fields, magma plumbing, eruption timing, or hotspots.

ABOUT THE EXPERIMENT

A useful approximation
of a restless planet.

Deep Time evolves an Earth-scale world using simplified rules for plate motion, rifting, oceanic crust creation and aging, subduction, island arcs, terrane accretion, continental collision, uplift, and erosion.

The input map marks oceanic crust, continental crust, and stronger cratonic cores. A random seed makes an experiment repeatable. One Myr is one million years.

This is a procedural, process-based tectonic model, not a solution of mantle convection or a reconstruction of real Earth. Its spherical mesh and rule choices set the detail and plausibility of the result. Review-map resolution changes the projection; tectonic mesh detail controls the simulation. A billion years is a scenario to explore.

Height maps use metres relative to the model's sea level. For 16-bit PNGs, elevation in metres equals the pixel value minus 12,000. The full experiment export includes the scientific grids, configuration, event history, and elevation encoding information.

Start with the Pangaea-like world, or paint an arbitrary distribution of continents and cratons. Run, scrub through time, and compare how each process leaves its mark.
